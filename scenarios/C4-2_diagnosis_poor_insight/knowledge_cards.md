## unawareness-in-hd
- Awareness of one's own actions and feelings appears to be impaired in at least a third of people with HD. [s36]
- Unawareness in HD differs from psychological denial. It is thought to come from disrupted frontal-basal ganglia pathways, and is sometimes called "organic denial" or anosognosia. [s36]
- A person may talk about specific problems (memory, movements, balance) without accepting that they have HD. Focus on those problems rather than repeatedly confronting the diagnosis. [s36]
- Insight may not be an achievable goal; some people never "accept" the disease. [s36]
- Unawareness can lead to anger when the person cannot understand why they are kept from work or driving, and can become dangerous. [s36]
- Give the diagnosis with a warning and in plain words, even when the person expects good news. [s20]
- It is the clinician, not the gene test, who judges that symptoms of HD are present; this matters when legal or financial decisions depend on it. [s35]

## work-and-money
- Losing the ability to do one's usual job is typically the first functional loss in HD. Many people can keep working for some time in a less demanding role. [s36]
- The decision to retire or apply for disability should be made with input from social workers and physicians. If employed, become familiar with the employer's disability plan. [s36]
- Social workers can help with disability, financial planning and applications such as CPP Disability or provincial supports. [s35]
- CPP Disability is a monthly benefit for people over 18 and under 65 who cannot work because of a disability and meet contribution rules. [s39]
- Discussing Power of Attorney early is important because unawareness can become dangerous. [s36]
- In British Columbia, an enduring power of attorney covers financial and legal affairs; a representation agreement is the only way to appoint someone for health care decisions. [s32]
- Each child of a person with HD has a 50% chance of inheriting the gene change. [s19]
- Predictive testing is reserved for adults, after careful discussion with a genetic counsellor. [s35, s6]
- In Canada, no one can be required to take a genetic test or disclose a result as a condition of a contract or service. [s7]
