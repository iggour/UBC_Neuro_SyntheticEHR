## talking-with-teenagers
- See the young person and parent together, and also give the young person time alone. [s24]
- Explain confidentiality and its limits early: what stays private, and when information must be shared to keep them safe. [s24]
- A parent in the room is likely to limit what a teenager says about sensitive topics. [s24]
- Ask permission before contacting the school or other services. [s24]
- Use a structured psychosocial check (HEEADSSS: home, education, eating, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide and mood, safety), starting with less sensitive topics. [s24]

## seizures-in-jhd
- Seizures are much more common in juvenile-onset than in adult-onset HD. Estimates range from about a quarter to over a third of people with JHD. [s17, s18]
- Seizures are more likely the younger the age of onset. Generalised tonic-clonic seizures are the most common type, and several types can occur in the same person. [s17]
- A first seizure needs its own work-up; never simply assume it is caused by HD. [s18]
- Anti-seizure medicines are used in JHD; there is little evidence to guide the choice. [s16, s17]
- Missed doses are a common reason for breakthrough seizures. Ask about them without judgement and solve problems with the prescriber. [?]

## seizure-safety
- Showers are safer than baths. Do not lock the bathroom door. [s23]
- Swimming only with supervision by someone who knows about the seizures and can help, such as a trained lifeguard, and with a buddy. Avoid diving. [s23]
- Seizure first aid: stay with the person and time it; move hazards away and cushion the head; once possible, turn them on their side; never restrain them or put anything in their mouth. [s22]
- Call 911 if a seizure lasts more than five minutes, another starts before recovery, it happens in water, breathing is difficult, or the person is injured. [s22]
- A written seizure action plan helps family, school and friends know what to do. [s22]
- An occupational therapist can assess safety at home and suggest equipment. [s18]

## school-and-friends
- Falling school performance is a recognised feature of JHD. Teachers may value a yearly cognitive assessment to plan a suitable curriculum. [s16]
- Most schools know little about HD. Classmates may not understand that the changes are caused by an illness. [s25]
- Students with JHD tire easily, and cannot simply "try harder". [s25]
- An individual education plan may need changing even within one school term, because JHD progresses unevenly. Accommodations can include extra time, seating, help with writing and adapted physical education. [s25]
- Staying in school with friends helps young people feel less isolated. [s25]
- Decide with the young person what is shared with the school and classmates. [s24]
- HDYO has information for young people and for schools: https://www.hdyo.org/
