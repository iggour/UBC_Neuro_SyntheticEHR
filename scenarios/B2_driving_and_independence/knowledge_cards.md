## driving-and-hd
- Driving safety should be discussed periodically with every person with manifest HD and their family. [s35]
- It is hard because driving is hard to predict in the office, it matters for independence and self-esteem, many people with HD lack insight into their driving, and families are reluctant to enforce a stop. [s35]
- Useful questions: recent crashes or tickets; whether family have noticed swerving, poor judgement or braking problems; whether family would let the person drive the grandchildren. [s35]
- Family members may fear the person's reaction and should be given a chance to speak to the clinician alone. [s35]
- In unclear cases, refer for a formal driving assessment, usually by an occupational therapist. A person may pass a controlled test and still be unsafe under stress. [s35]
- Advise stopping with care: emphasise concern for safety, explain that slowed reactions may not be visible to the person, and base the advice on history, examination and outside information. [s35]
- The clinician can take responsibility for the instruction, so the family's report is not the focus, but agree this with the family first. [s35]
- In British Columbia, clinicians must report a patient with a condition that makes driving dangerous who continues to drive after being warned. RoadSafetyBC makes the licensing decision. [s31]
- Plan alternatives early (family, friends, community transport), and recognise the person's decision to stop. [s35]

## staying-safe-at-home
- An occupational therapist can assess driving, home safety and daily activities. [s35]
- Ask about falls, stairs and cooking directly; people may hide incidents because they fear being moved. [?]
- Practical changes can include stove safety devices, a bedroom and bathroom on one level, rails, and help with meals. [?]
- Home and community care services in BC can include home support and, when needed, long-term care; a health authority assessment is the starting point. [s34]
- Care planning in HD involves rehabilitation, carer support and risk-sharing with the family. [s35]

## planning-ahead
- People with HD lose the ability to express complex wishes later in the illness, so plans should be made early. [s35]
- It is important to record, early, whether the person would or would not want a feeding tube, and why. [s35]
- People with HD benefit from naming a trusted person to make decisions when they no longer can. [s35]
- In British Columbia, a person can make a representation agreement and/or an advance directive. If no one is named, the health care provider chooses a temporary substitute decision maker in an order set by law, starting with the spouse. [s32]
- Share copies of the plan with family and health care providers. [s32]
