## behavioural-onset
- Almost all people with HD develop personality and behavioural changes (a "dysexecutive syndrome": apathy, irritability, impulsivity, obsessionality), with possible severe effects on marriage, work and finances. [s36]
- Subtle changes may start well before the movement disorder is obvious. [s36]
- Irritability and temper outbursts are among the most common complaints from families. Irritability can also be a symptom of depression, so assess mood. [s36]
- The first impairment in daily life is usually losing the ability to do one's usual job. [s36]
- Families may wish to speak with the clinician privately about irritability and outbursts. [s36]
- Outbursts can become violent. Safety comes first; carers should have a plan for leaving the house or calling the police if needed. [s36]
- Asking directly about suicidal thoughts does not increase them. [s12]
- Ask whether there are firearms at home. [s36]
- Depressed people should always be asked about alcohol and substance use. [s36]

## unknown-family-history
- Gene testing is particularly useful when the family history is unknown, for example after adoption. [s35]
- Diagnostic genetic testing should be used routinely in someone who appears to have HD but has no known family history. [s35]
- Knowledge of the typical age of onset sometimes leads clinicians to miss HD. [s36]
- People with HD are commonly unaware of mild involuntary movements. [s36]
- It is the clinician, not the gene test, who judges that symptoms of HD are present. [s35]
- A positive result in Mark would mean each of his children has a 50% chance of having inherited the gene change. [s19]
- In primary care, refer to neurology or an HD clinic when HD is suspected; genetic counselling is part of diagnostic testing. [?]
