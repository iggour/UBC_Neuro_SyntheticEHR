## imaging-findings-in-hd
- The typical MRI finding in HD is atrophy of the caudate head and putamen on both sides. [s47]
- This can enlarge the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles, giving a "boxcar" shape. General loss of brain volume can also be seen. [s47]
- MRI shows the caudate better than CT. [s47]
- Two ratios measure caudate loss: frontal horn width to intercaudate distance (normally 2.2 to 2.6, falls towards 1) and intercaudate distance to inner table width (normally 0.09 to 0.12, rises). They are not specific for a diagnosis. [s47]
- Brain imaging is often normal early in HD, but it can help to rule out other conditions. This statement comes from the juvenile HD chapter. [s49]
- In research studies, regional brain atrophy begins many years before diagnosable signs and continues steadily after them. [s48]
- Manifest HD should not be diagnosed from an MRI scan alone in a person without symptoms. [s49]
- Other causes of chorea can also show caudate or striatal loss, such as chorea-acanthocytosis and Huntington disease-like 2. [s51]

## what-each-test-can-and-cannot-tell-us
- Genetic test: confirms whether the HD gene change is present. It is not a way to diagnose when symptoms began. [s49]
- CAG ranges: 26 or fewer normal; 27 to 35 intermediate; 36 to 39 reduced penetrance; 40 or more full penetrance. [s1]
- In the 36 to 39 range, whether a person becomes ill and when is uncertain. [s28]
- Laboratories can differ when sizing the same sample, so read a size close to a range boundary with the laboratory's measurement error in mind. [s50]
- Brain MRI: can show a structural problem and may show caudate loss. It cannot confirm HD on its own, and a normal scan does not exclude early HD. [s47, s49]
- Routine blood tests: cannot secure a diagnosis of HD. They help to rule out other causes of abnormal movements, such as thyroid disease, drugs or toxins, and lupus. [s49]
- Further tests depend on the picture: a blood smear for acanthocytes and creatine kinase for chorea-acanthocytosis, ceruloplasmin for Wilson disease, antiphospholipid antibodies for antiphospholipid syndrome. [s51]
- Normal blood tests therefore do not show that HD is absent. [s49]
- Swallowing: a speech-language pathologist can assess swallowing and advise on safer textures. [s35]
- Clinical swallowing assessment adds to instrumental assessment (videofluoroscopy or endoscopy) but does not replace it. [s52]

## explaining-test-results-in-plain-language
- Before giving a result, ask what the person already understands and how much they want to know. [s20]
- Give a warning that difficult news is coming, then give information in small pieces and use everyday words. [s20]
- Genetic result, for example: "The test shows you carry the gene change that causes Huntington disease. It can't tell us how quickly things will change." [s49]
- Scan, for example: "One part of the brain looks a little smaller than expected. That fits with what we are seeing, but a scan alone can't tell us the cause." [general clinical practice]
- Normal blood tests, for example: "These tests look for other causes, like thyroid problems. They don't test for Huntington disease." [s49]
- Swallowing, for example: "Food and drink are going down the wrong way into her lungs. That is part of late-stage Huntington disease, and it causes the chest infections." [s35]
- Give only the numbers the person can use, and explain each one. [general clinical practice]
- Use teach-back: ask the person to say in their own words what they need to know, and explain again in different words if needed. [s53]
